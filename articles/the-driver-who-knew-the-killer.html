The Driver Who Knew the Killer

2026-11-12

Eddie Malone had driven Don Salvatore Romano for fifteen years, and in all that time the old man had never once asked him to wait outside a warehouse at midnight.

That was the thing Eddie could never forget about the night the Don died. Not the restaurant. Not the drive home. The warehouse. The Don had tapped twice on the glass between them — two taps meant stop, no questions — and Eddie had pulled into a dark lot off the highway, and a man had gotten into the back seat, and the Don had said, "Drive," and for eleven minutes nobody in that car said a single word.

The next morning, Salvatore Romano was dead. A heart attack, the doctor said. Sixty-eight years old, a bad heart, a lifetime of rich food and worry. The family buried him on a Friday, and his son Nico took over the business on Monday, and Nico told everyone the same story: he had been in Miami that night, a thousand miles away, closing a deal.

Eddie never told anyone about the warehouse. Drivers who talk do not stay drivers. They stay quiet, and they stay alive, and Eddie Malone had a wife and two daughters and a mortgage, so he stayed quiet for fifteen years.

Then Nico sold the old car.

It was a black sedan, older than Eddie's marriage, and the new owner's mechanic called Eddie because something was rattling in the glovebox. Eddie went down to the lot on a Tuesday afternoon, opened the glovebox the way he had opened it a thousand times, and found what fifteen years of driving had hidden from him.

A photograph. Creased. Faded. Tucked behind the car's manual, where no one ever looked.

It showed the old Don, Salvatore Romano, standing beside his car on the night he died. He was shaking hands with the man from the warehouse. And on the back, in the Don's own shaky handwriting, were the words that stopped Eddie's heart:

"If I die tonight, it was him."

Eddie turned the photograph over and looked at the man's face again. He knew that face. He had driven that face home from restaurants and airports and weddings for fifteen years. He had watched that face cry at the old Don's funeral, standing beside the coffin in a black suit, accepting condolences with tears in his eyes.

The man in the photograph was Nico Romano. The Don's own son. The man who was supposed to be in Miami.

Eddie sat in the driver's seat of the dead man's car for a long time, the photograph in his shaking hands, and the whole night came back to him in pieces.

The two taps on the glass. The warehouse. The man getting into the back seat. The eleven minutes of silence. And then — he remembered this now, and he wished he did not — the Don turning in his seat as the man got out, and saying, very quietly, "Nico. You would kill your own father?"

And Nico, standing in the dark lot, saying, "You were going to give it to Tommy."

Tommy. The younger son. The Don had been planning to name Tommy his heir, and cut Nico out, because Nico had been stealing from the family for years. Eddie had heard the Don say it once, on the phone, in the back of this very car: "Tommy has the head for it. Nico has the hunger. Hunger is not leadership."

So Nico had poisoned his father's medicine. A heart attack, the doctor said. A bad heart. Nobody checked the pills, because who checks the pills of a sixty-eight-year-old man with a bad heart?

Eddie put the photograph in his jacket pocket and drove home. That night he could not sleep. He thought about his wife and his daughters and his mortgage. He thought about the old Don, who had paid for his eldest daughter's wedding. He thought about Nico, who now ran the family, and who had killed his own father for an inheritance.

On Sunday morning, Eddie put on his best suit. He put the photograph in his inside pocket, next to his heart. And he drove to the restaurant where Nico Romano ate lunch every Sunday, at the same table, at the same time, like his father before him.

He walked in. The room went quiet. Nico looked up from his plate, and his face went white, because he recognised the driver, and he recognised the look of a man who had finally decided to stop being quiet.

Eddie sat down across from him, uninvited. He set the photograph on the table, face up, between the bread and the wine.

"Your father left you a message, Nico," Eddie said. "Eleven minutes in the back of my car. I remember every second of it."
